# The Last Student

Margaret had taught Marcus for nine years, and at the end of the lesson he stood up and said, "I think this might be my last one."

She had been writing in the notebook — *Inventions, hands separately, slow* — and she did not look up immediately. Marcus was seventeen and had grown four inches in the last year and the chair sometimes squeaked when he shifted, the way it had not squeaked when he was eight. He was holding the strap of his bag in both hands the way he had at his second lesson, when he had been nervous, and which she remembered because she had, at the time, written *good but tense* in a different notebook, a green one she had thrown away when it filled. There had been twelve notebooks since.

"Oh," she said. She closed the current one. "I see."

"I'm not — " He laughed, because he laughed when he was uncomfortable, and she had taught him the laugh was a tic and not a substitute for sentences, but he was seventeen and tics were what he had. "I'm going to college. There's a piano in the dorm but it's electric. I don't think I can — "

"You don't have to explain."

"Right."

"I mean it. You don't."

He nodded, three small nods, and she remembered that too, the small nods, from the first year. She had thought, when he was nine, that he would be the kind of student who quit at twelve, because the children with the quick fingers and the quick laughs almost always quit at twelve. He had not. He had stayed past twelve and past fourteen and past the year his father left, which she had not known about at the time but had figured out later from the way his playing had gone briefly to a place she recognized, quieter and more accurate, with a kind of steadiness that seventeen-year-olds did not, on average, possess.

"Will you play before you go?" she said.

"Like — what?"

"Whatever you want."

He sat back down. He thought about it for a long time, and she did not rush him, because she had learned that rushing the choice of piece was the quickest way to teach a student that the choice did not matter. After almost a minute he said, "The Bach. The C major."

"All right."

He played the C-major prelude from the first book, which was the piece she had been teaching for forty-one years, which she had taught to him when he was thirteen and which he had hated for two years and then, somewhere in the third year, stopped hating. He played it as he always played it now: a little too fast in the opening, the inner voices a little too pronounced, but with the small rocking quality in the middle section that she had spent a year showing him how to find. He did not look at the keyboard. He looked at a point on the wall just above the piano, which was the way her old teacher had played, and which she had told Marcus about once, in passing, when he was fifteen, and which he had quietly adopted.

When he finished he kept his hands on the keys for two seconds. She had taught him this too — *don't lift, let the room finish the piece for you* — and he had learned it. The room finished the piece. It was a Tuesday afternoon in early September and the light through the front window was the particular golden-brown of all the lessons she remembered with anyone, including the lessons she remembered with herself, when she had been Marcus's age and her own teacher had still been alive.

"Thank you," he said.

"For the lesson?"

"For — yeah. For all of them."

"You're welcome."

"I'll send you a letter when I get there."

"You won't."

"I will."

"Marcus, you absolutely won't, and that's fine, and I want you to know that's fine. Some of my students send letters. Most of them don't. I'm fond of all of them either way."

He laughed the laugh again. He stood up. He bent at the waist, which was new, which was a thing seventeen-year-olds with their height tried to do because their bodies were still working out how to occupy the space they now had, and he did not quite manage to make it look natural. She did not mention it.

He left.

She sat at the piano for a few minutes without playing. The notebook was open on the bench beside her. She had written *Inventions, hands separately, slow* twenty minutes earlier, when she had still believed there would be a next lesson, and she let the entry stay there. It would still be true if there was a next lesson. It would be a useful entry to have, somewhere, in case it came up again, with anyone.

Then she played the C-major prelude.

She played it the way she had taught Marcus to play it, which was a small lie, because she had not played it that way in fifteen years — she had her own way now, slower and with less rocking — and the way she taught it was an artifact of how she had played it in the year she had finally figured out how to teach it, which was a different year. It had become a teaching object, separable from her own playing. Today she played it the teaching way. She played it through twice, and the second time she let the bass line ring more than she usually did, because she was alone and there was no student to confuse.

When she had finished she stood up and went into the kitchen and made tea. While the kettle was on she looked at the schedule on the refrigerator. Marcus had been her four-thirty on Tuesdays. The slot had been his since he was twelve, when she had moved him out of the three-thirty children's slot. There were no other students after him on Tuesdays. There had been, ten years ago. There had been three after-Marcuses on Tuesdays, ten years ago, and the schedule had been reorganized twice since — first to consolidate, then to consolidate again, and the second time she had not added new students to fill the gaps because she was sixty-eight and her hands were no longer reliably warm by ten in the morning, and there had been a thing with her sister, and there had been a winter where she did not know if she wanted to keep teaching at all.

She had kept teaching. She had kept Marcus. She had not, looking back, kept him out of obligation. She had kept him because he had been the kind of student who became, over the years, something different from a student. She had been honest with him. She had not pretended he was a more talented student than he was. He had been, at his absolute best, an unusually attentive student of moderate gift, and his playing had become beautiful through that attention rather than through any extraordinary capacity. She thought this was actually the rarer thing. She had only had three or four students in her career who had made beautiful playing through attention rather than gift. She thought of them, often, with more affection than the gifted ones, who tended to disappear into ordinary professional lives in which the playing became one more competence among many.

The kettle whistled. She poured the tea. She took it back into the front room and sat in the chair across from the piano, not on the bench, and she drank it slowly while looking at the keyboard, which still had Marcus's slight indentations on the felt where he had pressed too hard during the opening of the prelude as a teenager, before she had taught him not to.

She did not feel sad. She had thought she might. She had thought, at certain moments in the last year, when she had begun to suspect Marcus would go to college and not come back, that the moment of his last lesson would be a moment with sadness in it, and she had tried to prepare for that. The preparation had been unnecessary. The actual moment had a different shape. The shape was: a student who had been a child when he came to her and was now a young man had played a Bach prelude, in his way, which was substantially the way she had taught him, and had said thank you, and had left, and now there was tea, and the light was the right color, and she was the one who had taught him.

She had taught him. She had done that. The thing was done. She did not need to do anything else with it.

She finished her tea. The light moved a little across the rug. She thought, briefly, about whether she should call someone — her sister, a friend — and decided against it. The thing did not need to be reported. The thing had happened, and she had been present for it, and she would, in a few minutes, get up and rinse the cup and start dinner.

She got up. She rinsed the cup. The kitchen window faced east and was already in shadow, and she stood at the sink for a few seconds longer than the cup required, with her hands in the warm water, and she thought of nothing specific and nothing general, only of the warmth, and of the fact that there was still a great deal of evening to come.
